Young Kyung Kim

Ph.D. candidate in Electrical and Computer Engineering at Duke University and visiting researcher at Princeton University, advised by Guillermo Sapiro.

I develop interpretable and monitorable AI systems that support human oversight and advance AI alignment. My research asks what monitorability means beyond language: if we can read how a model reasons in text, can we also follow how it makes an image or a 3D scene?

I introduced Chain-of-Image Generation (CoIG), to our knowledge the first systematic framework for monitorable image generation, and I’m extending this work to 3D generation. I also study how models can learn from the order of incoming data at test time, along with interpretability, structured reasoning, and clinical research on eating disorders and mental health.

Research

Monitorable generation and structured reasoning

Chain-of-thought lets people read how a language model reaches an answer. I ask whether other kinds of generation can be made just as legible. Chain-of-Image Generation produces an image through a sequence of inspectable semantic steps, and introduces readability and causal-relevance metrics that measure how well those steps reveal and influence the final image.

With CLARO, we use reinforcement learning to make language-model reasoning more readable and efficient by optimizing its structural attributes within a token budget. I’m now extending monitorability to 3D generation, so that people can oversee and intervene in how 3D content is made.

Papers: CoIG, CLARO. 3D work in progress.

Interpretability

Mixtures of Neural Additive Models (MNAM) capture several statistical relationships in the same data while keeping the readable feature-response functions that make additive models interpretable. Semantic Vision Transformers (sViT) use segmentation-based image tokens tied to meaningful regions, which makes visual representations easier to interpret and more robust to distribution shift.

Papers: MNAM, sViT

Learning from sequence order at test time

In a stream of real data, what comes next often depends on what came before. Order-Aware Test-Time Adaptation (OATTA) learns from that order: it estimates changing class-transition probabilities online from the model’s own unlabeled predictions, then refines predictions through Bayesian filtering without updating model weights. To our knowledge, it is the first general test-time adaptation framework to exploit latent class-transition matrices.

Paper: OATTA

Computational research in eating disorders and mental health

With clinical collaborators at Duke, I apply machine learning to eating disorders and mental health. We analyzed reflections on parent feeding strategies from 19,239 adults, where natural language processing classified perceived helpfulness with 82% accuracy. I’ve also studied facial behavior during food exposures for avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) and co-authored research on peer support for college mental health.

Papers: parent feeding strategies, repeated food exposures, peer support
